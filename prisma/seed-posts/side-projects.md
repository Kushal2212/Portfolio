I counted once. Since university I have started around forty side projects and finished fewer than half. Five have users I do not know personally. One pays for my coffee. The failure rate used to embarrass me. Now I think it is the most useful data I have.

## Finishing is the skill

Starting is cheap. A new repository has no legacy, no bugs and no disappointed users. Finishing means dealing with all three. The projects that shipped were not the ones with the best ideas. They were the ones where I defined "done" before writing code.

My definition is deliberately small now: a URL, a working core loop, a way to get help. No settings page. No onboarding tour. No dark mode in v1, which is a sentence I never thought I would write.

## Keep the scope small

The first version of my analytics side project had funnels, cohorts and a query builder. It never launched. The second version had one screen: page views over time, top pages, referrers. It launched in three weekends and got its first paying customer a month later.

Every feature you add before launch is a bet. A smaller launch tests the only question that matters at that stage: does anyone care?

## Distribution is part of the work

I used to treat marketing as the thing after the product. Now I treat it as part of the product. If nobody can find the thing, the thing does not exist.

What worked, in order:

1. **Writing about the problem.** Not the product, the problem. A post about why cookie-based analytics made me uncomfortable did more than any launch tweet.
2. **Shipping in public.** Small updates on the same platform, consistently. Predictability beat virality.
3. **Helping one person at a time.** The first ten users came from replies, not ads.

What did not work: posting a link into five communities on launch day, building a landing page before the product, and waiting until it was ready.

## Boring stacks ship faster

I like trying new tools. But every new tool is a decision I have to make twice: once now, once when it breaks. The projects that survived use boring, well-documented technology. The ones that died often died while I was debugging a build pipeline I had chosen for fun.

My default is the least interesting stack that can do the job. Excitement belongs in the product.

## Keep a graveyard

Every abandoned project gets a short note: what it was, why I stopped, what I would do differently. Reviewing that folder once a year is more useful than any retrospective on the projects that worked.

Some patterns:

- I was building for an imaginary user who wanted every feature.
- The scope grew faster than the proof.
- I was avoiding the hard part, usually distribution, by polishing the easy part.
- I had stopped using it myself.

## A different scoreboard

Social media rewards launches. The scoreboard I care about is quieter: did I learn something I can reuse, did I make something a few people love, did I leave the code better than the template?

Forty projects in, my advice is unglamorous. Pick smaller, ship sooner, talk about the problem, and keep a graveyard. The graveyard is where the next good idea usually comes from.
